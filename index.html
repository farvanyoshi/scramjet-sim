Scramjet Flow Lab

A supersonic-combustion ramjet flying at Mach 6, 30 km up. Air enters faster than sound, gets squeezed by shock waves, mixes with hydrogen and burns without ever slowing to subsonic. Turbulent boundary layers, wall friction and wall heating are all simulated.

What you are looking at

  1. Inlet

    Two 9° ramps and the cowl lip bend the air. Each bend makes an oblique shock, a thin sheet where pressure and temperature jump. At Mach 6 the air slows to about Mach 3.2 and heats from 227 K to roughly 650 K in the core. A turbulent boundary layer thickens along the walls, where friction slows the air.

  2. Isolator

    A straight duct where shocks bounce between the walls. Where a shock hits a boundary layer, the slow air near the wall can separate. If the combustor pushes back too hard, that separation runs forward and the inlet unstarts.

  3. Combustor

    Sonic hydrogen jets fire from slots in the floor and the cowl (dashed box). The fuel has to mix with the air by turbulence before it can burn, and it only self-ignites where the air is hot enough. The flow stays supersonic while it burns: that is the “s” in scramjet.

  4. Nozzle

    The floor drops away at 16° so the hot, high-pressure gas expands and speeds up. Pressure on that sloped wall makes the thrust. Friction on every wall and the heat soaking into the structure work against it.

Things to try

  • Drop to Mach 4.5. The air never gets hot enough for hydrogen to self-ignite, so the engine only makes drag.
  • Switch the flow model to Inviscid. Friction and wall heating vanish and thrust jumps. That gap is what real engine designers fight.
  • Open Turbulence. The bright bands are turbulent boundary layers and mixing layers. They grow along every wall.
  • Push φ past 1. There is more fuel than oxygen to burn it. Thrust barely creeps up while the share of fuel actually burned falls below half, and the combustor exit nears Mach 1 (thermal choking).
  • Fly at Mach 10. The hydrogen burns instantly, but friction drag and a huge wall heat load eat the thrust.
  • Hover over the flow to read Mach, temperature, pressure, speed and fuel at any point.

How it is computed

The solver integrates the 2D Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations with a finite-volume method: MUSCL reconstruction with a van Leer limiter, the HLLC Riemann solver for the shocks, central differences for viscous stresses and heat conduction, and two-stage Runge–Kutta time steps. Viscosity follows Sutherland’s law at the real Reynolds number of the flight condition (about 2 million per metre).

Turbulence uses the Spalart–Allmaras one-equation model, the standard in aerospace CFD. The grid is too coarse to resolve the thin layer right at the wall, so wall functions (Spalding’s law of the wall and the Reynolds analogy) give the skin friction and heat flux, with the walls cooled to 1,000 K. On the first ramp the friction lands within about 15% of the Van Driest II correlation.

Air is thermally perfect: its specific heat rises with temperature as N2 and O2 start to vibrate. Hydrogen burns in a one-step reaction with an Arrhenius rate. It needs oxygen, releases 120 MJ/kg, and stops releasing heat near 2,900 K, where water starts to dissociate.

Simplified: 2D, averaged turbulence rather than resolved eddies, one-step chemistry, the mixture is treated as air, and the fuel jets are spread over their penetration height instead of resolved. Treat the numbers as a teaching approximation.
